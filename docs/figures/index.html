Coastal geography · Santa Cruz, California

Pleasure Point — a measured field guide

What the coastline actually does, what the seabed under it looks like, and how those two facts decide where a swell breaks. Public survey data throughout, plus a work-in-progress simulation built on it.

01 The setting

Pleasure Point is a rocky point on the east side of Santa Cruz, California, at roughly 36.954°N, 121.976°W, where the coastline turns a corner and runs east toward Capitola. The shore is a low marine terrace — a cliff of about 10–12 m fronted by a shallow wave-cut rock platform — and the surf breaks over that platform rather than over sand. Access is by stairs at 38th and 41st Avenues.

Two things make the site unusual. Monterey Bay gives it a wide swell window, so it works year round: winter west swells arrive refracted and attenuated around the bay, while summer south swells aim more directly at the point. And because the coast rotates through the corner, a single incoming swell meets the shore at a steadily changing angle, producing a series of distinct named breaks along roughly 1.6 km of shoreline.

The place was named in the 1920s for a Prohibition-era speakeasy above the break; the surfing association came later. This guide is concerned with the first meaning of the name only in passing — the rest is geometry.

02 The corner the coast turns

Local surf guides all state the same qualitative rule, which turns out to be a usable description of the physics:

“the waves get larger, faster, and more powerful as you move up the point” — Sunny California, A Beginner's Guide to Surfing Pleasure Point

That's a folk rule until you put a coastline polyline through it. Pulled from OpenStreetMap and walked as arclength from the apex, Pleasure Point's coast tangent swings roughly 110° over about 550 m — precisely, −54.8° at Little Wind-an-Sea to +57.3° at First Peak, a measured 112° turn. That's the point, mechanically: the corner the coast takes between Sewers and First Peak.

Past First Peak the tangent barely moves — it wobbles around a mean of roughly 45–47° all the way to Private's, cusped by the little coves at the Hook. So angle alone cannot explain the golden rule's down-point softening. The map establishes the turn; the next figure adds the two fields that transform one incoming swell along it: shelter and reef elevation.

03 The down-point gradient

A storm raises the whole point, but not by the same multiplier. Every spot receives the same offshore conditions; exposure, reef depth, and tide decide the local result. On a small day the active point contracts toward the exposed upper breaks. As swell builds, more of the sheltered coast switches on, while First Peak and Sewers become the fastest, heaviest expressions of the same ocean.

The result is a spatial sequence of breaking regimes rather than a single wave. Private's and Sharks sit in the most sheltered water and require both swell and a low tide to activate at all; the Hook and 38th occupy intermediate exposure; Second Peak sits on a low-gradient shelf and produces the long, roughly 90 m peel the site is best known for; First Peak's steeper profile yields tapered, faster walls; and Sewers, at the apex, is the most exposed and shortest-period-tolerant of the seven. One swell, seven outcomes, set by position along the corner.

04 Breaking character along the point

The first two figures describe one measured coastline. The third changes evidence: these are simulations. The renderer spans peel angles from α = 70° to 35° — from a feathering, barely spilling shoulder to a hollow, heavily sectioned, closeout-prone lip.

The Pleasure Point canon is settled: Sewers, First Peak, Second Peak, 38th/Jack's, the Hook, Sharks, and Private's. The renderer still uses a legacy character bank in which Cowell's, Middle Peak, and the Slot are West Side archetypes. Retargeting that bank — so location comes from u and the geo profile, while storm conditions remain global — is model work, not a naming decision. Until then, these frames claim wave character, not geography.

05 The simulation work in progress

Everything above is measurement. This section is not: it is a real-time WebGL model built on that measurement, and it is unfinished. It is included because it makes the central claim of this guide testable — if the shape of the seabed sets where a wave breaks, then replacing the seabed should visibly change the wave.

The model is kinematic, not a fluid solver. A phase travels along a break line; the seabed supplies water depth, and depth supplies the rest: shoaling by Green's law (Ks = √(cg0/cg)), depth-limited breaking at H = min(H0Ks, γh) with γ ≈ 0.78, and a shoreline wherever depth crosses zero. It reproduces plausible behaviour; it is not validated against measurements of this break, and nothing here should be read as a forecast.

The clearest test is a counterfactual. Below, the same swell runs over the same site twice: once over the surveyed seabed, and once over that seabed's own least-squares plane — identical mean depth, slope and orientation, with only the structure removed (2.6 m RMS at Second Peak). The reef is the only variable that changes.

The model exposes the mechanism directly as a cross-section: seabed, still-water level, the shoaling height H0Ks, and the depth ceiling γh. Breaking is where the last two cross. Moving the tide slides that crossing point along the profile while the breaking depth stays fixed — γ sets the depth, the bed decides where that depth occurs.

Known limitations

  • Not validated. No comparison against measured wave heights, breaking positions, or imagery of this break has been made.
  • Four of seven sites are mapped. Second Peak, First Peak, The Hook and 38th (Jack's) use surveyed profiles. Three entries in the character bank still carry names from Santa Cruz's west side and run on a synthetic seabed; they are labelled as such in the app.
  • The break line is authored, not derived. Peel angle is still an input. A break line that emerges from the angle between the swell and the measured contour is the next substantive step.
  • Tidal datum is approximate. Depths are NAVD88; the conversion to local sea level (0.905 m) comes from NOAA station 9413450 at Monterey, about 40 km away, because the Santa Cruz gauge publishes no NAVD88 relationship.
  • Underwater visibility is exaggerated for legibility. Real visibility here is a few metres.
  • Wave height is exaggerated relative to the terrain, deliberately: true heights are close to invisible at landscape scale.
